The pilgrim's manifesto

Modern life surrounds us with financial instruments, insurance policies and digital vaults, yet anxiety over basic survival remains sharper than ever. Camino Henro is a pilgrimage on foot across a whole continent, from Portugal to Japan, made to investigate one question.

What is the true philosophical foundation for building a sense of material security in human life?

On the Weight of a Rucksack & the Gift of the Road

by Henro 遍路

Essay 1. Written before setting out, Portugal. 8 minutes.

其の一

Sample text for the prototype. Henro's own essay will replace it.

Before a pilgrim takes the first step eastward toward the rising sun, every object in his possession is put on trial by gravity. In a stationary life, an extra coat, a third pair of boots or an unused ledger costs nothing to keep in the corner of a room. On the road from Portugal to Japan, every gram demands metabolic tribute.

The night before leaving, everything goes on the floor and each object answers one question: is it worth carrying across a continent? Most things fail. What passes fits inside forty liters of canvas.

同行二人Walking together, two as one. On the trail, material security is found in the rhythm of the step and the unexpected fruit offered along the way.

We are taught to equate security with accumulation, an ever higher wall of assets between ourselves and contingency. The paradox of the long-distance pilgrim is that security grows in direct proportion to what one can relinquish while remaining whole.

其の二

A ledger of grams

A rucksack is an honest ledger. It records every decision as weight and presents the bill at the first long hill. A house keeps no such account. Things settle into cupboards and stay there for years without ever asking to be carried.

The first week on foot edits a pack more severely than any packing list. Whatever has gone unused for seven days is usually given away on the eighth, and the shoulders register the gift before the mind does.

其の三

The gift of the road

The other half of the answer arrives from outside the pack. A stranger fills a water bottle. A farmer points to a fig tree and says to take what is ripe. In Shikoku this has a name, osettai, and it is offered to the pilgrim as a matter of course.

A pack that is light enough leaves a hand free to receive. That may be the first working definition of material security this walk can offer: enough to keep moving, and a free hand.

Two Staves, One Path: Why the Camino Meets Shikoku

by Henro 遍路

Essay 2. Written in Astorga, Spain. 11 minutes.

其の一

Sample text for the prototype. This essay has its own photograph under the scroll, so keep scrolling past the end.

Two roads have taught people to walk for centuries. One runs west across Spain to the tomb of Saint James. The other circles the island of Shikoku through eighty-eight temples in the footsteps of Kūkai. They share almost nothing in doctrine and almost everything in practice.

Both give the walker a staff, a hat against the weather and a mark that says pilgrim to anyone who sees it: a scallop shell on the Camino, a white robe and a sedge hat in Shikoku. Both rely on the same economy. The walker arrives with little, and the people along the road make up the difference.

The crest of this walk puts the two together on purpose. The staff of the Camino and the staff of the henro are crossed under one hat, with the shell between them. The hat carries four characters, 同行二人: two travelling together.

The Hospitality of Strangers as Economic Bedrock

by Henro 遍路

Essay 3. Written on the Meseta, Spain. 9 minutes.

其の一

Sample text for the prototype. Henro's own essay will replace it.

Somewhere on a long road a stranger hands a walker a piece of fruit. Nothing is asked in return, and both go on their way. Economists have a poor vocabulary for this moment. Pilgrims have a precise one.

In Shikoku the gift is called osettai, and giving it is understood as a way of taking part in the pilgrimage without leaving home. On the Camino the same instinct runs through the donativo hostels, where a bed is offered and each guest leaves what they can.

A safety net made of such gestures holds no reserves and publishes no accounts, and it has carried walkers along these roads for centuries. This essay asks what that says about the systems that do hold reserves.

問答

Questions and answers

What people ask along the way.

問答
問Why walk from Portugal to Japan?
答Portugal and Japan sit at the two ends of the Eurasian landmass. Walking from one to the other joins the Camino de Santiago to the eighty-eight temples of Shikoku in a single road.
問What does the crest mean?
答It sets the sedge hat of the Shikoku pilgrim, marked with a Sanskrit seed syllable, above the scallop shell and the cross of Saint James. Two crossed pilgrim staves lie beneath them.
問How are essays published from the road?
答Henro writes on a phone or a library computer and presses publish. The essay appears here about a minute later.
問What happens to donations?
答Every gift goes to food, shelter and worn-out gear. Each euro in or out is written in the finances ledger.
